The founder

TCO Technology is closing the gap between paperwork and the giants.

Mike CrimaldiFounder, TCO TechnologyHighland Heights, Ohio

We're new. We never imagined we'd be building software, but we were inspired and encouraged to take the leap.

I went to work for a friend's construction company. Six people, good at the work, and the accounting software everybody uses. Running the company was still a mess.

I tried the other systems. None of them fit. What I noticed was that every big job moves through the same sequence of stages, start to finish, and none of the software was built around that.

We began with one idea for crews in the field. The more I looked, the clearer it got: almost nobody was building tools for the businesses in the middle. That's how TCO Technology started.

We're based in Highland Heights, just outside Cleveland, Ohio.

We're a new company, and we're looking at this market through a new lens.

Almost everyone gets overwhelmed by a very large task. The answer is the one painters use to copy a masterpiece: work in sections, one square at a time, and the whole thing comes together.

Ask the owner whether their software works. They'll tell you it works for them. Now ask the crew. That's the gap we're closing: software that works for the crew, not only the office.

So our tools are built in sections too. Each stage is something you can see, finish and hand off, so nobody has to hold the whole job in their head.

Research and rationale

How we got here, and why we built it this way.

The journey

It started with one idea for field crews, not a company. The more we looked at how businesses actually run day to day, the clearer it got that the middle of the market had been left out.

Big platforms were built for large teams with IT departments. Simple tools stop where a job gets complicated. Everything since has been about closing that gap.

We're building in the United States, so the research here is American unless we say otherwise. The one exception is a UK industry report, used because it's the closest match to field work we could find.

The rationale
Interruptions cost everyone.

In research on interrupted work, people made up the lost time by working faster, and paid for it in stress and frustration. Every crew pays that cost.

Fast Company, 2008, interview with Gloria Mark, University of California, Irvine
Neurodivergent people are already on construction crews.

A 2023 UK industry report found that about one in four construction workers identify as neurodivergent, and close to 40% of them had not told their employer.

National Federation of Builders, CITB and The People's Partnership, 2023
ADHD is common among U.S. adults.

The CDC reported in 2024 that about 15.5 million U.S. adults, roughly one in 16, have a current ADHD diagnosis.

Staley et al., Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report, 2024
Attention costs money at work.

A study of one large manufacturing company linked adult ADHD to a 4 to 5% drop in work performance. It's one firm and one industry, so we present it as a signal, not a rule.

Kessler et al., Psychological Medicine, 2009
